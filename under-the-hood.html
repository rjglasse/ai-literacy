Under the Hood

Before you read this page, you'll see a few questions appear. Don't skip them. The point isn't to get them right — it's to make you think before you're told what to think.

What actually happened during training

A large language model is built by training software on an enormous volume of text — web pages, books, forums, code, articles, conversations — far more than any person could read in a lifetime. That text was not curated for accuracy; it was assembled at scale, so the system learned from good arguments and confident misinformation alike.

Through billions of iterative adjustments, the system was trained to do one thing well: predict which word (or token) is most likely to follow a given sequence of text.

That is the entire training objective. The model is not a database of verified facts, not a reasoning engine, not a knowledge store. It is a large set of learned statistical patterns derived from text.

Tokens, not words

The model technically operates on tokens — subword units roughly the size of a syllable or short word — but the key point is the same either way: it is doing pattern matching. When you ask a question, it generates text that statistically resembles a plausible response, based on everything it was trained on. It is not retrieving a stored answer or working through a logical chain of reasoning.

The output often looks deliberate and well-informed because competent-sounding prose appeared frequently in the training data — not because the system possesses the competence it appears to demonstrate.

Why it sounds so confident

The model was optimised for plausibility, not accuracy. These are different objectives.

The system has no internal mechanism for verifying its claims against reality. When it produces a fabricated citation, an invented date, or a confident but incorrect assertion, it does so with the same fluency it applies to well-evidenced statements. The field calls these errors hallucinations, though the term is more dramatic than the cause: the model simply follows a probable linguistic trajectory that happens to diverge from the truth.

This is not a malfunction. It is the system working as designed.

What the model doesn't have

Your knowledge is grounded in continuous lived experience — you know things because you have a body, memory, and a history of interacting with a world that provides real feedback. The model has none of this. It has no persistent memory across conversations, no sensory experience, and no way to check whether what it says is true.

What it does have is an uncanny ability to produce text that resembles expertise. That resemblance can be genuinely useful — and consistently misleads users who mistake fluency for reliability.

Why this matters

Plausibility is not correctness. Fluency is not accuracy. The appropriate response to a well-written LLM output is not trust — it is engaged scepticism: knowing which errors are likely, developing habits of verification for consequential claims, and treating generated text as a starting point rather than a conclusion.
