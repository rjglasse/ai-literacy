<question type="mcq" header="The unit distinguishes between &quot;durable skills&quot; and &quot;tool-specific skills.&quot; Which of the following is the best example of a durable skill?
">
  <q-option reason="Interface knowledge is tool-specific: it is tied to a particular product that may be redesigned, discontinued, or superseded within a year or two. Investing in interface fluency is useful in the short term but does not transfer when the tool changes.
">Knowing how to use the interface of a particular AI assistant</q-option>
  <q-option reason="Prompting skill has real near-term value, but it is partially tool-specific: what works for current LLMs may not work for architecturally different future systems. The underlying skill of communicating clearly and specifying requirements precisely is more durable than any particular prompting technique.
">Writing effective prompts for the current generation of large language models</q-option>
  <q-option correct reason="Correct. This is a durable skill because the need to judge outputs — for accuracy, appropriateness, and fitness for purpose — does not depend on which tool produced them or how the tool works. It transfers across every generation of AI systems, and it is the same critical reading skill that is valuable in any domain involving information and communication.
">Evaluating whether a piece of generated text is accurate, appropriate, and fit for purpose</q-option>
  <q-option reason="This knowledge has a very short shelf life: the best-in-class model today will typically be superseded within months. Staying current on the landscape has some value, but it is a surface-level activity that does not build the deeper competence needed to work well with whatever tools emerge next.
">Knowing which AI tools are currently considered best-in-class</q-option>
</question>


<question type="mcq" header="What is the most useful way to distinguish a credible AI trend from speculation?
">
  <q-option reason="The source is not a reliable guide. Academic researchers have made wildly optimistic predictions about AI timelines; industry researchers have produced rigorous, cautious analyses. What matters is the quality of the reasoning and evidence, not the affiliation of the person making the claim.
">Credible trends come from academic researchers; speculation comes from industry</q-option>
  <q-option reason="This standard is too conservative: it would classify all forward- looking analysis as speculation, including well-grounded predictions supported by strong mechanistic evidence. The distinction between credible and speculative is about the quality of the reasoning, not whether the event has already occurred.
">Credible trends have already happened at scale; speculation is about what hasn&#x27;t happened yet</q-option>
  <q-option correct reason="Correct. The key markers of a credible claim are: an identified mechanism by which the change would occur, a track record of predictions from this source being borne out, and evidence that holds up under scrutiny. Speculation characteristically works by projecting current rates of change forward — &quot;things have been accelerating, so they will keep accelerating&quot; — which has a poor historical record as a forecasting method, even for fast-moving technologies.
">Credible trends are supported by evidence about mechanisms and track records; speculation relies on extrapolation from current rates of change</q-option>
  <q-option reason="Consensus is not the same as credibility. Multiple sources can converge on a speculative claim if they are all relying on the same questionable reasoning or extrapolating from the same flawed assumptions. Some of the most accurate forecasts about technology have initially been minority views; some of the most widely shared predictions have been badly wrong.
">Credible trends are predicted by multiple sources; speculation is a minority view</q-option>
</question>

<panel header="LLM Excursion" type="seamless">

**Task:** Open an AI tool and have a three-turn conversation about the future of your field. Turn 1: Ask how AI might change the field you want to work in over the next five years. Turn 2: Push back on its answer — ask what evidence supports its claims and what it might be getting wrong. Turn 3: Ask it what skills in your field are most likely to remain valuable regardless of how AI develops. Record all three exchanges.


**Capture:**

- tool_used
- prompt_used
- output
- one_surprise
- one_thing_to_verify

**Reflect:** How did the AI's responses change when you pushed back? Did it become more cautious, more specific, or did it simply agree with your challenge? What did the three-turn structure reveal that a single question wouldn't have? Which of its claims about durable skills matched your own instincts, and which surprised you?


<box type="info">The three-turn structure is deliberate: it moves from broad prediction to evidence evaluation to practical advice. Notice how the conversation quality changes when you ask the AI to justify and refine its claims.
</box>
